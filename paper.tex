\begin{abstract}
Open-source software (OSS) has become the foundation of modern software development, with 96\% of codebases containing OSS components. However, a significant portion of these projects face sustainability challenges: 91\% of codebases contain components with no development activity in the past two years. This study investigates factors that differentiate sustainable from non-sustainable OSS projects through a comparative sample study of 500 GitHub repositories (250 sustainable, 250 non-sustainable). We analyze governance practices, community health indicators, and ecosystem metrics to identify predictive factors. Our results show that maintainer guidelines (OR=4.22, p<0.0001), bus factor (median 4 vs. 2, p<0.0001), and stars threshold (>11,934 = 4.15x lift) significantly predict sustainability. A combined XGBoost model achieves 97.2\% prediction accuracy (AUC=0.993). We conclude that governance maturity and contributor structure predict sustainability better than popularity metrics alone, providing actionable insights for maintainers, contributors, and organizations evaluating OSS dependencies.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Problem and Motivation}

Open-source software (OSS) has become the foundation of modern software development. According to recent industry reports, 96\% of codebases contain OSS components~\cite{synopsys2024}. However, a significant portion of these projects face sustainability challenges: 91\% of codebases contain components with no development activity in the past two years~\cite{synopsys2024}. This creates substantial risk for organizations depending on OSS.

The problem matters for several reasons. First, security vulnerabilities in unmaintained projects remain unpatched, creating attack vectors. Second, breaking changes in dependencies cascade through ecosystems. Third, organizations invest in projects that may become abandoned, leading to costly migration efforts.

This problem affects multiple types of practitioners:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{OSS maintainers} need guidance on governance practices that promote longevity
    \item \textbf{Contributors} must assess project health before investing effort
    \item \textbf{Organizations} need to evaluate dependency risks
    \item \textbf{Funders} require evidence-based criteria for supporting projects
\end{itemize}

The practical context appears when practitioners must decide which projects to depend on, contribute to, or fund, but lack clear indicators of long-term sustainability.

\subsection{Research Questions}

We address the following research questions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1:} What governance and documentation practices differentiate sustainable from non-sustainable projects?
    \item \textbf{RQ2:} How do community health indicators (response times, bus factor, contributor diversity) correlate with survival?
    \item \textbf{RQ3:} Do ecosystem metrics (stars, forks, watchers) correlate with sustainability?
    \item \textbf{RQ4:} Which factors best predict sustainability when combined?
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Contributions}

This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item First study combining governance, community, and ecosystem dimensions in one predictive framework
    \item Novel operationalization of bus factor and maintainer guidelines metrics
    \item Interpretable ML model achieving 97.2\% accuracy with practical thresholds for practitioners
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{OSS Sustainability}

Crowston and Howison~\cite{crowston2005} established foundational metrics for OSS community structure, finding that successful projects exhibit specific communication patterns. Chengalur-Smith et al.~\cite{chengalur2010} conducted a longitudinal study defining sustainability through project lifespan and activity patterns. Coelho and Valente~\cite{coelho2017} analyzed why modern open source projects fail, identifying common patterns of abandonment.

\subsection{Governance and Documentation}

Schweik and English~\cite{schweik2012} analyzed factors leading to project success, identifying governance maturity as critical. The OpenSSF Scorecard~\cite{openssf} operationalized security and maintenance metrics but focuses on security rather than sustainability. O'Mahony and Ferraro~\cite{omahony2007} studied the emergence of governance in open source communities.

\subsection{Community Dynamics}

The CHAOSS project~\cite{goggins2021} developed standardized community health metrics. Avelino et al.~\cite{avelino2016} formalized the ``truck factor'' (bus factor) concept, measuring key-person risk. Constantinou and Mens~\cite{constantinou2017} studied socio-technical evolution in the Ruby ecosystem. However, these metrics lack validation against actual sustainability outcomes.

\subsection{Ecosystem and Popularity}

Borges et al.~\cite{borges2016} examined factors driving GitHub popularity, noting stars often reflect marketing rather than quality. Recent work by He et al.~\cite{he2024} revealed millions of fake GitHub stars, challenging popularity-based evaluations.

\subsection{Research Gap}

Prior work typically examines single dimensions (governance OR community OR ecosystem). No study has: (1) combined all three dimensions in one predictive framework, (2) validated metrics against ground-truth sustainability labels, or (3) provided interpretable thresholds for practitioners.

\section{Research Method}

\subsection{Study Design}

We conducted a comparative sample study using repository mining, following the ABC framework~\cite{stol2018}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Setting:} Neutral (analyzing existing data without manipulation)
    \item \textbf{Strategy:} Sample study examining distribution in population
    \item \textbf{Generalization:} Statistical (sample to GitHub population)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Data Collection}

We collected data in three phases:

\textbf{Phase 1: Governance metrics.} Using the GitHub API, we checked for presence of CODE\_OF\_CONDUCT, CONTRIBUTING.md, LICENSE, issue templates, PR templates, README, MAINTAINERS.md, GOVERNANCE.md, and CODEOWNERS files.

\textbf{Phase 2: Community metrics.} Using GHArchive, we extracted median issue response time and PR review time. Using the GitHub Contributors API, we calculated bus factor (minimum contributors accounting for 80\% of commits) and contributor Gini coefficient.

\textbf{Phase 3: Ecosystem metrics.} Using the GitHub API, we collected stars, forks, and watchers counts.

The final dataset contains 32 features across 500 repositories.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Implications of Findings}

\textbf{Governance maturity matters more than documentation breadth.} Maintainer guidelines (OR=4.22) outperform contributing guides (OR=1.94). Maintainers should prioritize explicit governance documentation (MAINTAINERS.md, GOVERNANCE.md) over generic contribution guidelines.

\textbf{Contributor structure predicts longevity.} A bus factor of 4 indicates sustainable leadership distribution. Contributors should assess whether key-person risk exists before joining a project.

\textbf{Stars are necessary but not sufficient.} The threshold effect at approximately 12,000 stars shows diminishing returns. Organizations should not rely solely on star counts for dependency decisions.

\textbf{Combined models outperform single-dimension evaluations.} The 97.2\% accuracy demonstrates that multi-factor assessment is superior. All stakeholders should adopt holistic evaluation frameworks.

\subsection{Recommendations for Practitioners}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Maintainers:} Add MAINTAINERS.md or GOVERNANCE.md early in project lifecycle
    \item \textbf{Contributors:} Check bus factor >3 and issue response times <1 day before joining
    \item \textbf{Organizations:} Use combined metrics for dependency evaluation, not just stars
    \item \textbf{Funders:} Prioritize projects with explicit governance documentation
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations and Threats to Validity}

\textbf{Internal validity.} Our binary sustainability classification may oversimplify reality; we mitigated this by testing three alternative definitions (all showed robust results). Additionally, 47\% of OpenSSF scores were missing; we addressed this via MICE imputation and sensitivity analysis.

\textbf{External validity.} Results are limited to GitHub projects and may not generalize to GitLab, Bitbucket, self-hosted repositories, or non-software projects. Our 500-star minimum threshold means findings apply to already-visible projects; early-stage or niche projects may exhibit different patterns.

\textbf{Construct validity.} This is an observational study. All findings represent predictive associations, not causal relationships. We cannot claim that adding governance documentation \textit{causes} sustainability---only that it \textit{predicts} it. Correlation may arise from confounding factors (e.g., skilled maintainers implement both governance AND sustain projects).

\section{Conclusions}

We analyzed 500 GitHub projects across governance, community, and ecosystem dimensions to identify factors associated with OSS sustainability. Our main findings are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Maintainer guidelines} show the strongest governance association (OR=4.22, p<0.0001)
    \item \textbf{Bus factor} of 4 differentiates sustainable from non-sustainable projects
    \item \textbf{Stars threshold} of approximately 12,000 provides a meaningful sustainability indicator (4.15x lift)
    \item \textbf{Combined prediction} achieves 97.2\% accuracy, demonstrating multi-dimensional assessment superiority
\end{enumerate}

For practitioners: document governance explicitly, build core maintainer teams (bus factor $\geq$4), don't trust stars alone, and use interpretable models for dependency risk assessment.

Future work should examine whether implementing recommended practices causally improves sustainability through longitudinal studies, and extend the analysis to non-GitHub platforms.
